01 · The context

It is hard to imagine today that a software company would need convincing to invest in mobile.

In 2011, that was exactly the conversation I was having.

I had joined a small enterprise SaaS company serving the retail merchandising industry. The company had recently landed a much larger enterprise customer and was building out product, engineering, and QA capabilities to support the account. I was brought in as the product function. There had not previously been a formal product organization, so I was establishing the processes, workflows, and product practices while also leading the platform itself.

The platform coordinated a large distributed workforce working across major retailers throughout the United States. Regional, district, and store managers scheduled and managed the work. Field workers went into stores to complete assignments such as resetting shelves, building displays, conducting product demonstrations, and other merchandising work.

The technology reflected an earlier era. Administrators used a very old desktop application to manage the underlying organization and workforce structure. A newer Silverlight application supported scheduling, store and job information, and other manager workflows.

Field workers, however, performed their actual jobs inside stores.

That disconnect immediately stood out to me.

02 · Seeing the opportunity before it became a request

The natural place to document field work was where the work was happening.

Field workers needed to document what they had completed, answer questions associated with an assignment, and provide evidence that the work had been done. Without a practical mobile experience, that meant completing the physical work first and then returning to a desktop-oriented system to report it afterward.

That did not make much sense to me.

By 2011, smartphones were becoming more common, and bring-your-own-device practices were beginning to gain traction. I believed the natural place to document field work was where the work was happening.

A worker who had just rebuilt an end cap should be able to answer the related questions and take the required photo while standing in front of it. Asking that worker to remember the details and recreate the work later from a desktop added an unnecessary step and separated the evidence from the moment it was created.

The customer was not pushing us for a mobile application. The existing product still worked, and mobile was not yet assumed to be part of every enterprise software experience.

I kept advocating for it anyway.

03 · Making the case for an investment without an immediate ROI story

The investment was about remaining relevant as the way people worked changed.

I raised the mobile opportunity repeatedly for roughly two years.

The response was generally some version of: What we have works. Why do we need to spend money building something new?

That was a reasonable financial question, but I thought the bigger product risk was being missed.

I could not promise that building a mobile application would create an immediate financial return. My argument was that remaining dependent on a desktop experience for a field-based workforce would eventually make the product feel outdated and make the company less competitive.

The investment was about remaining relevant as the way people worked changed.

Over those two years, mobile adoption continued to grow and the idea became easier for the organization to see. Eventually I received approval to move forward.

At first, it was almost a pet project. I began working through what the product should be, how existing workflows might translate to mobile, and what a mobile experience could enable that the desktop product could not.

04 · Researching the work where it actually happened

I knew the answer could not simply be to put the entire existing application on a smaller screen.

I went through the Silverlight product screen by screen and workflow by workflow, asking a basic question:

Does this make sense in the field?

I also spent time with the people who would actually use it.

I visited field workers at retail locations including Costco and Sam's Club and talked with them while they were working. I worked closely with our enterprise customer. We also held a large working session with store and district managers from multiple locations so I could better understand their workflows, frustrations, and what would genuinely be useful away from a desktop.

There was no established internal mobile expertise or product playbook to follow. We were learning the space as we built it.

That made direct observation and customer conversations especially important.

05 · Deciding what belonged on mobile

The first priority was to make field work easier to complete and document.

The first priority was straightforward: make it easier for field workers to complete and document their jobs while they were doing them.

The initial experience allowed workers to:

  • View their assignments
  • Open the questionnaires associated with a job
  • Record the information required to show that the work had been completed
  • Capture and upload photographic evidence

As the product evolved, I added workflows for store and district managers, including scheduling and approval-related functionality that benefited from being available while managers were away from a desk.

I deliberately left other functionality out.

Complex administrative work, organization of store hierarchies, and heavyweight reporting still made more sense in the desktop application. Mobile did not need to contain every feature simply because the feature existed elsewhere.

The goal was to make the work easier. Reproducing the entire desktop product on a phone would not have done that.

06 · Designing the experience myself

I handled the product design work as well.

We did not have a UX designer, so I handled the product design work as well.

I started with rough wireframes and moved into more detailed mockups and interaction flows using Snagit. I shared early versions broadly with the internal team, including offshore developers.

Some of the most useful feedback came from those developers. They had strong ideas about how the interface could feel more modern, and I incorporated that feedback as the designs evolved.

I owned the product direction, but that didn’t mean every good idea had to come from me. I listened to the feedback, changed the designs where it made sense, and the product got better because of it.

07 · Designing for a field environment that was not reliably connected

A mobile application for field workers could not depend on having a strong signal every moment.

One of the largest practical problems was connectivity.

Today it is easy to forget how inconsistent mobile connectivity could be inside large retail stores at that time. A mobile application for field workers could not depend on having a strong signal every moment.

That changed the product design.

I worked through which parts of an assignment and questionnaire needed to be available for offline completion. Workers needed to be able to download the information required for the job, complete their work even when connectivity disappeared, answer the associated questions, and capture photos as evidence.

The application then needed to retain that work and synchronize it when connectivity returned.

The flow became:

Download assignment → enter store → complete work offline if needed → answer questions → capture evidence → regain connectivity → synchronize completed work

That capability was essential. Mobile only solved the original problem if it worked in the actual conditions where field employees were expected to use it.

08 · Building something new required the whole team to learn

Mobile was also new territory for engineering and QA.

Mobile was also new territory for engineering and QA.

The development team had to learn how to build responsive experiences across different mobile operating systems and device types. QA had to expand its testing approach and accumulate a range of devices so we could see how the application behaved under different conditions.

We ultimately supported native iOS, Android, and Windows applications, which reflected the mobile environment at the time.

Throughout development, I led the requirements and worked directly with engineering and QA. When QA found an issue that behaved differently across devices or operating systems, I would have them walk me through exactly what they were seeing so I could understand the experience and adjust the product where needed.

I was also the final acceptance pass. After QA completed its testing, I personally reviewed the completed functionality before approving it for release.

09 · Launching to the workforce

The customer immediately understood how much easier the experience could make field work.

Before launch, I presented the product to the enterprise customer during extended working sessions. The response was extremely positive. They immediately understood how much easier the experience could make field work.

We did not run a formal pilot or beta. We had done extensive product definition and QA, and the initial release focused on a very practical problem that workers already understood.

The customer's internal team handled communication to its workforce. I reviewed those communications, corrected product details where needed, and helped make sure employees were being given accurate instructions.

Support followed an escalation model. Field workers contacted the customer's support organization first, and more complex questions, defects, suggestions, and product needs were escalated to us. Those inputs became part of the product intake process I established and continued to manage.

10 · What changed

The value of the product became very clear once it was in use.

Field workers no longer had to finish their work and then sit down later to reconstruct what they had done. When connectivity was available, reporting could happen immediately. When it was not, completed work could synchronize after the worker left the store and regained service.

Evidence was also captured closer to the actual work. Photos and answers were being recorded in context rather than recreated later from memory.

Field workers embraced the experience because it removed work from their day rather than adding another system they were required to use.

We continued building on the mobile product over the following years, adding functionality for additional user groups and improving the experience as devices and connectivity improved.

Mobile eventually supported the platform's largest user population and became the most important part of the product offering.

The application that had originally required years of advocacy became central to how the product delivered value.

11 · Looking back

Looking back now, the resistance to mobile is almost funny.

Today we expect software to work from a phone. We conduct business, communicate, manage workflows, capture information, make purchases, and do work from devices we carry everywhere.

In 2011, that future was visible, but it was not yet obvious to everyone.

I think about this experience often as I work with AI today.

The technologies are completely different, but the product instinct feels familiar. A meaningful shift is happening in how people work. Customers may not yet know exactly what to ask for. Existing workflows still function, so it can be tempting to wait until the need becomes unavoidable.

My experience with mobile taught me to pay attention earlier.

It also taught me that seeing where things are going isn’t enough. I still had to do all of the product work to make the idea useful.

I still had to go into stores, talk with users, understand the existing workflow, decide what belonged on mobile, design the experience, work through unreliable connectivity, help engineering and QA navigate unfamiliar territory, validate the product, and keep improving it after launch.

That experience gave me more confidence in my product judgment.

It also shaped the way I approach emerging technology today: learn it early, understand where it genuinely improves the work, and then do the practical product work required to turn that opportunity into something people can actually use.

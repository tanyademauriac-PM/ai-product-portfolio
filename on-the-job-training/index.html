This case study describes work for a highly regulated energy-sector enterprise SaaS platform. Company and customer names, internal names, proprietary screens, financial values, and confidential measurement thresholds have been removed. Operator Qualification, or OQ, is the regulated process used to confirm that workers can perform specific covered tasks safely and correctly.

01 · The Opportunity

Five signals pointed to a larger product opportunity.

Customers in a highly regulated energy sector had been requesting on-the-job training capabilities for several years.

By the time I began exploring the opportunity, five signals had converged:

  • longstanding customer demand
  • a highly manual field workflow
  • competitor functionality already in market
  • customer renewal and retention relevance
  • a natural commercial fit alongside existing training and qualification products

At least one major customer was also paying for a separate apprenticeship platform to manage part of the same workflow. Together, these signals pointed to a larger product opportunity.

02 · The Existing Workflow

The current process was extremely manual.

Field employees learning hands-on skills could carry large binders containing the competencies they needed to demonstrate. A qualified expert would observe the employee performing the work, find the relevant competency, document the result, and sign off.

Organizations supplemented that process with paper, spreadsheets, internal tools, and in some cases third-party platforms.

The result was a fragmented workflow with significant administrative effort, greater opportunity for human error, and limited connection to the digital system already used to manage training and qualification.

03 · Customer Discovery

Six real customer implementations shaped the product model.

I began with an initial concept of what an OJT capability might require, then validated the real workflow with the people responsible for running qualification programs.

I conducted structured discovery with OQ administrators at six of our largest enterprise customers.

The six organizations had solved the problem in different ways, but the core workflow was remarkably consistent. Administrators defined the skills employees needed to demonstrate. Employees learned through supervised practice. Qualified mentors or experts observed performance and confirmed competency.

The details varied.

In some cases, the companies had directly opposite requirements. Some companies wanted mentors to record hours, repetitions, or comments as part of competency signoff. Others deliberately avoided collecting certain details because unnecessary data could create additional questions during an audit.

That discovery led to one of the most important product decisions in the initiative:

Standardize the workflow. Configure the policy.

The product needed one scalable OJT workflow with company-level settings where legitimate operational and regulatory differences existed.

See the discovery details

The research focused on:

  • how OJT worked inside each organization
  • who participated in the process
  • how competencies were created and assigned
  • who could verify proficiency
  • what evidence organizations recorded
  • where the existing process created friction
  • which workflow elements were consistent across companies
  • where company policy or audit practices required different behavior
04 · Developing Domain Understanding

Discovery expanded my understanding of the product space.

The discovery process surfaced an important domain insight. Formal training and qualification were only part of how these employees learned. For hands-on field work, supervised practice was a critical part of skill development.

I expanded the research beyond our customers to understand established OJT models, industries where structured OJT and apprenticeship are common, how these programs have evolved, specialized apprenticeship and training platforms, and where a focused OJT capability naturally belonged within an enterprise qualification product.

That research helped define both the immediate product and the longer-term product boundary.

05 · Commercial Product Opportunity

The opportunity had a credible commercial path.

Customers were already paying for enterprise training and qualification software. The same workforce participating in qualification programs also needed supervised, hands-on learning. At least one major customer was already paying separately for a specialized platform supporting part of that workflow.

I recommended placing OJT behind its own subscription entitlement and permission structure from the beginning.

That gave us controlled access during early validation and created a clear path toward future monetization as the product matured.

Formal pricing would be established later through the appropriate commercial leadership process. My responsibility was to establish that the customer value, product fit, usage potential, and commercial logic justified further investment.

06 · Defining the MVP

The MVP provided a complete usable OJT workflow and a way to learn from real-world adoption.

I defined the MVP to implementation-ready detail.

An administrator could create objectives, define eligible mentors, assign objectives, and give apprentices clear visibility into the work. A qualified mentor could observe the employee, confirm demonstrated competency, and capture hours, repetitions, or comments when company policy required them.

The MVP delivered the smallest complete workflow required to test the product hypothesis.

How I defined the MVP

The first release focused on the complete workflow that could deliver useful value and generate meaningful evidence. I deliberately deferred capabilities that belonged in later phases:

  • apprentice-entered hours and repetitions
  • more sophisticated signoff models
  • advanced reporting
  • scheduling
  • richer administrative controls
  • expanded editing and lifecycle management
  • additional workflow automation
07 · Planning Beyond the MVP

The MVP was one phase of a larger product strategy.

I also created a future-phase product Idea designed to make the OJT offering substantially more robust and valuable across the broader customer base. That future state was broken into multiple epics so the product could continue expanding in controlled stages.

The roadmap separated immediate validation needs from the capabilities required to build a more mature and commercially valuable product.

See the future product strategy

Future capabilities included apprentice-entered hours and repetitions, more sophisticated signoff models, advanced reporting, scheduling, richer administrative controls, expanded editing and lifecycle management, and additional workflow automation.

Organizing the future state as a larger Idea split into multiple epics created a path toward a much more robust product with greater value for customers that use OJT and substantial future revenue potential.

08 · Business Case and Approval

I translated the research into a formal business case.

The business case connected customer demand, the competitive gap, existing workflow pain, repeatability across major customers, natural fit with the existing product ecosystem, and future subscription potential.

The business case received product-leadership approval and moved into implementation. The initiative also became part of formal quarterly planning and OKRs.

09 · Limited Launch and Success Measures

The planned introduction was intentionally controlled.

The six enterprise customers who participated in discovery were the strongest initial cohort because we already understood their workflows, requirements, and expectations.

I created a detailed, high-touch limited-launch plan. We would selectively enable and support each participating customer, observe real workflow behavior, identify friction, measure adoption and completion, evaluate repeat usage, gather qualitative feedback, and use that evidence to prioritize the next product phase. Broader access would follow after the product demonstrated sufficient value.

Success was defined before release.

Success measures were established as part of the business case and reinforced through quarterly product objectives. The framework focused on product behavior and adoption.

Initial adoption

Are participating organizations creating and beginning to use OJT objectives within the expected period?

Workflow completion

Are assigned objectives progressing through observation and competency signoff?

Repeat engagement

Do administrators and mentors return and continue using the product?

Breadth of usage

Does adoption expand beyond isolated trial activity?

Qualitative validation

Can customers complete their real OJT workflow successfully, and what additional capabilities are needed next?

The exact internal thresholds are confidential. The measurement framework was designed to validate adoption, repeat usage, workflow fit, and readiness for further expansion.

How I planned the limited launch

The plan was designed to:

  • selectively enable participating customers
  • support each implementation closely
  • observe actual workflow behavior
  • identify friction
  • measure initial adoption
  • measure completion of the core workflow
  • evaluate repeat usage
  • gather qualitative feedback
  • determine priorities for the next product phase
  • expand access after the product demonstrated sufficient value

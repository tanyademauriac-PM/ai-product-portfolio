Product Failure, Post-Launch Metrics & Roadmap Adaptation

A Product Launch That Fell Short

Metrics, Stakeholders, Engineering, and the Pivot

Shortly after joining an enterprise SaaS company, I inherited a long-requested Saved Filters feature that had been stalled in discovery for several months. I was asked to take over the initiative, course-correct the stalled discovery, and see it through implementation and launch.

01 · One Launch, Several Product Leadership Challenges

An underperforming launch can surface several leadership challenges at once.

This launch made me stop and examine several product leadership questions.

Where did it fail?

Where was I wrong?

Were the metrics I used the right ones or would others have been more useful?

Should we have pivoted early on?

Two symptoms were pointing to the same product outcome failure. Adoption was lower than expected because the organization was still developing its GTM discipline and the launch did not have enough enablement behind it. At the same time, the feature did not deliver enough value for some administrators because an enhancement that would have reduced reporting effort further was technically constrained and deliberately excluded.

01

Where did it fail?

The feature shipped, but adoption and reuse were lower than I expected.

02

Where was I wrong?

I was so focused on solving the delivery problem I inherited that I underestimated the launch and adoption problem.

03

What metrics did I use, and what would have been more useful?

After launch, I measured adoption, Saved Filter creation, configuration behavior, reuse, and session behavior.

Those metrics helped expose the problem. In hindsight, I would have established clearer behavioral success measures and adoption expectations before launch so we could evaluate the outcome against an explicit product hypothesis rather than simply observing usage afterward.

04

Should we have pivoted earlier?

Not before we had evidence.

The feature solved a real problem and worked as designed. The important decision was not to abandon it prematurely, but to use post-launch evidence to determine whether additional investment was justified.

Once the adoption data and customer feedback told us it was not, I knew the right answer would be to change the roadmap.

05

How did I handle difficult stakeholder conversations?

When customer-facing teams continued bringing us the same request for the enhancement we had excluded because it would not work with the existing infrastructure, I investigated it again rather than simply repeating an old answer.

When the technical constraint remained, I explained that clearly, kept the customer need visible, and avoided promising something we could not responsibly deliver.

06

How did I work with Engineering when something went wrong?

I understood during discovery why the requested enhancement could not work with the existing infrastructure. Each time the request resurfaced, I went back to Engineering to ask whether the technical conditions had changed.

The constraint was a product reality to manage. I kept the customer need visible without pushing Engineering toward an implementation the infrastructure could not responsibly support.

02 · What Actually Failed?

Two signals pointed to the same disappointing product outcome.

Signal 01

Adoption was lower than expected.

Our product organization was still developing stronger go-to-market practices, and the launch did not have enough enablement behind it.

I had been heavily focused on rescuing a stalled initiative and getting it delivered.

discovery → delivery became discovery → delivery → adoption

That part of the outcome was mine to own.

Signal 02

The feature did not reduce enough customer effort.

Customers repeatedly requested an enhancement that would have made Saved Filters more useful and eliminated more of the work involved in running reports.

We had deliberately excluded that capability because our principal engineer had identified a technical constraint.

I could own the GTM weakness without pretending I could magically remove a legitimate engineering constraint.

We successfully delivered the capability, but it did not create as much customer value or adoption as I expected.
03 · How I Knew the Outcome Was Falling Short

The quantitative evidence showed what was happening. The qualitative evidence helped explain why.

After launch, I worked regularly with our data team to understand what administrators were actually doing with the feature.

We tracked how many administrators created Saved Filter sets, which filters they saved, how many sets they created, and how often they returned and reused them.

I also reviewed session behavior in LogRocket.

Together, those signals showed lower adoption and reuse than I had expected.

But the quantitative evidence only told me what was happening.

The qualitative evidence helped explain why.

Account managers repeatedly brought us the same customer request for an enhancement that would have made Saved Filters more useful and reduced more of the effort involved in running reports. What was that enhancement request? You guessed it: it was the functionality that was technically constrained.

That feedback gave context to what we were seeing in the behavioral data.

04 · Managing Stakeholders and Engineering Through the Problem

I kept revisiting the technical constraint as the customer need resurfaced.

The enhancement customers started to request post launch had been deliberately excluded because of a technical limitation identified by our principal engineer.

But I did not treat the original answer as permanent.

Each time the request resurfaced, I went back to Engineering.

I asked whether the technical conditions had changed and whether there was now a responsible way to support the request.

When the answer remained no, I communicated that clearly to customer-facing stakeholders, documented the unmet need, and kept it available for future reconsideration.

01 I did not pressure the team into an implementation they believed was technically irresponsible.
02 I did not promise stakeholders functionality we could not reliably deliver.
A technical constraint can make the answer “not now,” but it should never make the customer need disappear.
05 · The Pivot

The feature did not need to be rolled back. The investment decision needed to change.

This was not a situation where rollback was the right response.

The feature worked.

Some customers were receiving value from it.

Removing it would not have solved the underlying problem.

The investment decision was what needed to change.

I had originally planned to extend reusable Saved Filters into additional reporting workflows.

But the adoption data and customer feedback did not justify expanding the capability at that point.

So the roadmap had to change.

Rather than continuing to invest because we had already spent time building the feature, or because I wanted the original hypothesis to prove correct, we prioritized other customer problems with stronger evidence of value.

Original plan Extend reusable Saved Filters to additional reports
→
Post-launch evidence Adoption and customer value did not justify expansion

06 · What I Got Wrong

I was so focused on solving the delivery problem I inherited that I underestimated the launch and adoption problem.

The experience changed how I think about launch success.

I had been asked to rescue an initiative that had spent months in discovery, and much of my attention naturally went toward narrowing the problem, establishing scope, creating momentum, and getting it delivered.

I did those things successfully.

But shipping was not the outcome.

I learned to treat adoption planning, behavioral success measures, launch enablement, and post-launch validation as part of the product itself, not activities that begin after development ends.

08 · The Failure, Plainly

I shipped it.

The adoption data didn't support the outcome I expected.

Customer feedback explained why.

I took responsibility for the GTM weakness.

I repeatedly challenged whether the technical constraint still applied without pushing for an implementation the infrastructure could not responsibly support.

And when the evidence didn't support further expansion, I prioritized higher-value work instead.

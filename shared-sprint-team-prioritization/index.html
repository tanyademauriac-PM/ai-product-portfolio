Cross-Initiative Product Prioritization

Prioritizing Competing Initiatives Across Shared Sprint Teams

How I revised a delivery plan when discovery revealed that two legitimate initiatives could no longer fit within the same planning cycle.

The product decision

Protect the validated customer-facing MVP while keeping the internal workflow initiative moving wherever capacity allowed.

About this case study

This is based on an actual enterprise SaaS planning and delivery cycle. Initiative names and selected identifying details have been changed to protect confidential company information. The product challenges, decisions, actions, and outcomes described reflect the work I led.

01 · The Planning Collision

New scope made the original plan unrealistic.

The product organization had multiple sprint teams to assign work to, but much of the available engineering capacity was already committed to other necessary work. The sprint teams were shared across product leadership, so two initiatives could compete for time on the same sprint team even when they were led by different product leaders.

I owned New Customer Capability (Project NCC), a net-new customer-facing workflow designed to bring a fragmented, largely manual customer process into the enterprise platform. Another product leader owned Internal Workflow Automation (Project IWA), an enhancement designed to automate an existing recurring internal administrative workflow.

Both were planned against the same sprint team. Initial high-level assumptions suggested that Project IWA would require only a handful of stories. Detailed discovery and story decomposition revealed significantly more complexity, expanding the work to nearly 20 user stories. The original expectation that both initiatives would fit within one planning cycle no longer matched the backlog or the team's capacity.

02 · Why Project NCC Came First

The priority followed the evidence and delivery readiness.

Project NCC had gone through direct discovery with several large customers, advisory-panel and roundtable validation, deliberate MVP definition, business-case approval, and detailed planning before delivery. Normal engineering complexity still surfaced, but its core MVP boundary remained substantially stable as engineering began.

That gave us stronger evidence and greater scope certainty when the capacity conflict emerged. Once Project IWA's true size became clear, the case for protecting Project NCC was stronger. The other product leader agreed that Project NCC should receive protected priority.

03 · Reworking the Plan

I turned the priority decision into a workable sequence.

I raised the capacity risk with higher leadership early and brought the actual backlog, Project IWA's newly understood scope, Project NCC's remaining work, dependencies, and available sprint-team capacity into the discussion. We aligned on Project NCC as the protected priority.

Working rule Project NCC first. Project IWA continued when dependencies, engineering skills, or remaining capacity made concurrent progress possible.

I owned the broader Product Backlog and product-level prioritization, while the Product Owner owned the Sprint Backlog. We worked through the planned work together so sprint-level sequencing reflected the agreed product priorities.

I moved unrelated nice-to-haves and noncritical defects back to the broader backlog and protected Project NCC from accumulating unplanned work. Genuinely urgent items entered only when their impact justified displacing planned work. I moved a small number to another sprint team when limited knowledge transfer made that feasible.

With the Engineering Lead, I mapped dependencies, identified work that could run concurrently, checked whether larger stories needed further breakdown, and sequenced foundational work before dependent work. I stayed close to sprint plans across both cycles and participated in planning sessions to make sure the agreed priority continued to hold as dependencies and normal delivery constraints changed available capacity.

04 · Outcome

Both initiatives reached implementation completion.

The tradeoff was real: work we originally expected to complete in one planning cycle ultimately required two. That wasn’t the outcome anyone planned for, but once the new scope was understood, the original timeline was no longer realistic. My focus shifted to making the best use of the capacity we actually had: protecting Project NCC’s complete MVP while keeping Project IWA moving where possible. Both initiatives reached implementation completion by the end of the second cycle.

05 · What I Carried Forward

Strong discovery improves downstream planning reliability.

I validate uncertain scope earlier and involve engineering sooner when sizing or dependencies are unclear. I preserve meaningful buffer for normal delivery variance and require an explicit tradeoff when new work enters a full plan. Strong discovery gives the team a more reliable starting point, while normal engineering uncertainty still needs active planning.

Practices I kept
  • Earlier scope validation with engineering
  • Buffer for normal delivery variance
  • Explicit tradeoffs when plans are full

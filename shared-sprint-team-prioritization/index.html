Cross-Initiative Product Prioritization

Prioritizing Competing Initiatives Across Shared Sprint Teams

How I revised a delivery plan when discovery revealed that two legitimate initiatives could no longer fit within the same planning cycle.

The product decision

Protect the validated customer-facing MVP while keeping the internal workflow initiative moving wherever capacity allowed.

01 · The Planning Collision

New scope made the original plan unrealistic.

The product organization had multiple sprint teams to assign work to, but much of the available engineering capacity was already committed to other necessary work. The sprint teams were shared across product leadership, so two initiatives could compete for time on the same sprint team even when they were led by different product leaders.

I owned New Customer Capability (Project NCC), a net-new customer-facing workflow designed to bring a fragmented, largely manual customer process into the enterprise platform. Another product leader owned Internal Workflow Automation (Project IWA), an enhancement designed to automate an existing recurring internal administrative workflow.

Both were planned against the same sprint team. Initial high-level assumptions suggested that Project IWA would require only a handful of stories. Detailed discovery and story decomposition revealed significantly more complexity, expanding the work to nearly 20 user stories. The original expectation that both initiatives would fit within one planning cycle no longer matched the backlog or the team's capacity.

02 · Why Project NCC Came First

The priority followed the evidence and delivery readiness.

Before delivery, I had conducted direct discovery with several large customers and used customer advisory-panel and roundtable discussions to validate the workflow problem. I defined the MVP, developed the business case, secured higher-leadership approval, and completed detailed requirements and story planning.

Normal engineering complexity still surfaced, but Project NCC's core MVP boundary remained substantially stable. The work completed upstream significantly reduced scope uncertainty going into delivery. Project NCC also addressed the stronger customer and product need relative to Project IWA's newly understood cost, and implementation had already begun. The other product leader agreed with prioritizing Project NCC.

03 · Reworking the Plan

I turned the priority decision into a workable sequence.

I raised the capacity risk with higher leadership early. I brought the actual backlog, Project IWA's newly understood scope, Project NCC's remaining work, dependencies, and real sprint-team capacity into the discussion. We agreed on the working rule: Project NCC first. Project IWA would continue when Project NCC was blocked by a dependency, the available engineering skill set allowed both to progress, or capacity remained after Project NCC work was covered.

I owned the broader Product Backlog and product-level prioritization, while the Product Owner owned the Sprint Backlog. We worked through the planned work together so sprint-level sequencing reflected the product priorities and capacity decisions I was accountable for.

I moved unrelated nice-to-haves and noncritical defects back to the broader backlog and protected the Project NCC MVP from accumulating unplanned work. Truly urgent work could still enter when customer blockage, regulatory exposure, severity, or a direct dependency justified displacing planned work. A small number of urgent items moved to another sprint team when limited knowledge transfer made that safe.

With the Engineering Lead, I mapped dependencies, identified work that could run concurrently, checked whether large stories needed further breakdown, and sequenced foundational work before dependent work. I participated in planning sessions and stayed close to the sprint plans across both cycles. Available capacity also reflected normal delivery constraints, including shared specialists, dependencies, engineering review, and team availability.

Working rule Project NCC first. Project IWA continued when dependencies, engineering skills, or remaining capacity made concurrent progress possible.
04 · Outcome

The one-cycle assumption became a realistic two-cycle plan.

The revised plan protected Project NCC's complete MVP without abandoning Project IWA. Lower-priority work moved out, and both initiatives progressed around actual dependencies and engineering capacity.

Both initiatives reached implementation completion by the end of the second planning cycle.

Protected scope Project NCC's complete MVP stayed intact
Continued progress Project IWA remained active in usable capacity
Realistic plan Two planning cycles replaced the one-cycle assumption
Implementation Both major initiatives reached completion
05 · What I Carried Forward

Strong discovery improves downstream planning reliability.

I validate uncertain scope earlier and involve engineering sooner when sizing or dependencies are unclear. I preserve meaningful buffer for normal delivery variance and require an explicit tradeoff when new work enters a full plan. Strong discovery gives the team a more reliable starting point, while normal engineering uncertainty still needs active planning.

Practices I kept
  • Validate scope earlier
  • Involve engineering sooner
  • Preserve meaningful delivery buffer
  • Make new tradeoffs explicit

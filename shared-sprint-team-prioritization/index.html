Cross-Initiative Product Prioritization

Prioritizing Competing Initiatives Across Shared Sprint Teams

I used customer evidence, scope discipline, and shared-capacity planning to protect a validated MVP after the true size of a second initiative changed the delivery plan.

The product decision

01 · The Planning Collision

New scope made the original plan unrealistic.

The product organization had multiple sprint teams to assign work to, but much of the available engineering capacity was already committed to other necessary work. Multiple product leaders also shared sprint-team capacity. Two initiatives could be competing for time on the same team even though each had a different product owner.

I owned Project Fieldstone, a significant customer-facing workflow capability. Another product leader owned Project Relay, an internal workflow-automation initiative that would also establish useful product and platform capability. Both were legitimate priorities, and both were planned against the same sprint team.

Initial high-level assumptions suggested Relay would require a handful of stories. Detailed discovery by the Product Owner revealed substantially more complexity. Relay grew to nearly 20 user stories. The original expectation that both initiatives could be completed in one planning cycle no longer matched the backlog.

I recognized the mismatch before the plan failed through execution. There was no large pool of available engineering capacity elsewhere, so the next decision had to be about priority and sequencing.

02 · Why Fieldstone Was the Priority

Fieldstone entered delivery with a validated problem and a controlled MVP.

Before development began, I had completed substantial product work on Fieldstone. I conducted direct discovery with several large customers, used advisory-panel and roundtable discussions to test the problem, defined the MVP, developed the business case, secured approval, and translated the plan into detailed requirements and stories.

That upstream work made the delivery plan more reliable. Normal engineering complexity still surfaced, but Fieldstone's scope remained substantially stable. The initiative did not multiply in size once development started because the problem, MVP boundary, and detailed requirements were already well understood.

Fieldstone also addressed a validated customer need, had already begun implementation, and had an approved business case. I recommended protecting its MVP once capacity became constrained. The other product leader agreed with that priority based on the evidence. Relay remained important and would continue wherever capacity allowed.

Customer evidence Direct discovery with several large customers
Advisory input Roundtables reinforced the workflow problem
Product definition A deliberately defined and approved MVP
Delivery readiness Business case, requirements, and story planning complete
03 · Making the Tradeoff

I brought the actual backlog into the decision.

I raised the capacity risk with higher leadership early. I showed the current backlog, Relay's newly understood scope, Fieldstone's remaining MVP work, and the delivery constraints affecting the shared sprint team. The other product leader was part of the discussion, and we aligned on Fieldstone as the primary initiative.

At this point, I owned the broader Product Backlog and product-level prioritization. The Product Owner owned the sprint team's Sprint Backlog. I kept that ownership clear and worked closely with her so the sprint-level sequence reflected the agreed product decision.

Working rule Fieldstone first. Relay used remaining capacity when Fieldstone work was dependency-blocked or when available engineering skills allowed both initiatives to advance concurrently.

New work required an explicit tradeoff.

I used practical, contextual judgment for the requests competing with the plan. I considered whether a customer was blocked, whether there was regulatory exposure, how severe the issue was, whether it was required for Fieldstone, whether another sprint team was better positioned to handle it, and what would happen if it waited until the next planning period.

A small request could still enter the plan when it was genuinely urgent, severe, or safe to move elsewhere with limited knowledge transfer. Every addition had to make the cost to committed work visible.

04 · Turning the Decision Into Execution

The priority had to remain visible in the sprint plans.

I worked directly with the Product Owner through the full planned backlog. Together we put Fieldstone first, moved unrelated nice-to-haves and noncritical defects back to the broader backlog, and protected the MVP from accumulating unplanned work.

I also worked closely with the Engineering Lead to understand dependencies and the engineering skills each story required. We identified work that could run concurrently, checked whether large stories needed further breakdown, and sequenced foundational work before dependent stories.

I stayed close to the sprint plans across both planning cycles and participated as product leadership in the team's planning sessions. That helped keep the agreed priority intact as PTO, review needs, dependencies, validation, and normal rework changed the available capacity. A small number of genuinely urgent items moved to another sprint team when the transfer was safe and the knowledge burden was limited.

Usable capacity reflected real team constraints
  • Shared UX and principal engineering capacity
  • Engineering review and development-lead bandwidth
  • PTO, dependencies, and engineering skill differences
  • Normal rework and validation
05 · Outcome

The one-cycle assumption became a defensible two-cycle plan.

The capacity mismatch was recognized early, before the team was left to discover it through missed work. The priority decision was explicit, the Sprint Backlog reflected it, and unrelated work was deferred.

Fieldstone and Relay both advanced through deliberate sequencing around dependencies and available engineering skills. Both major initiatives were completed by the end of the second planning cycle.

06 · Product Leadership Takeaway

Strong discovery improves planning reliability.

Fieldstone's scope stayed controlled because substantial customer discovery, validation, MVP definition, business-case development, and detailed planning happened before delivery. That preparation gave us a reliable basis for the priority decision when capacity tightened.

I carried several practices forward from this cycle. I involve engineering earlier when size or dependencies are uncertain, challenge assumptions that work will be small, preserve meaningful buffer in later sprints, and require an explicit tradeoff when new work needs to enter a full plan.

I also stay closer to execution on strategically important work while preserving the Product Owner's sprint-level ownership. Shared capacity requires ongoing product judgment. A priority only protects the outcome when it continues to shape the backlog and sprint plans.

What I carried forward
  • Validate scope earlier
  • Bring engineering into uncertain sizing sooner
  • Protect committed work from accumulated small requests
  • Keep buffer for dependencies and normal delivery variance
  • Make the tradeoff explicit when priorities change

Work Triage: Better Product Decisions Through Rapid Prototyping

I use rapid, working prototypes to turn ambiguous business problems into decisions a team can evaluate. They help me test workflow behavior, uncover difficult handoffs and decide what deserves further validation before committing significant engineering time. This Work Triage example shows that approach in action.

01 · The Situation

A large caseload can hide the work that needs attention today.

In this fictional example, a case manager handles dozens of active matters. Client updates, pending document requests and follow-up commitments live in different parts of the workflow. The case manager has to piece those details together to decide where to focus each day.

Team leads need a clear view of which commitments are moving, where someone is waiting on another role and which follow-ups have stalled.

02 · The Challenge

Finding an issue doesn't automatically tell someone what to do next.

An exception list might help case managers find more problems, but it could also become one more queue to interpret. Seeing a warning is only useful if the person can understand the evidence, decide on the next step and keep that work from disappearing.

Responsibility complicates the workflow. A case manager may recognize that an attorney's input is needed, but that doesn't mean the attorney has accepted the request. And a case can be waiting on one dependency while another issue requires immediate attention.

03 · How I Approached It

I designed around the case manager's decisions, not the entire case-management system.

I started with one question: What does the case manager need to recognize, decide and record to keep the right work moving? That gave me a manageable workflow to prototype instead of an open-ended plan to rebuild case management.

The case manager is the primary user. Attorneys and paralegals retain responsibility for work within their roles, while an Operations view shows the status of recorded actions. I defined the scenarios, response options, responsibility boundaries and functional checks, then used AI coding assistance to build the interactive experience.

04 · The Key Product Decision

I chose a complete response workflow over another list of alerts.

I considered starting with an exception list showing which cases might need attention. That could reduce the time spent searching, but it would still leave case managers sorting through alerts and deciding what to do next. Instead, I focused the MVP on a small, connected sequence:

01RecognizeSurface a specific need, not a vague case status.
02PrioritizeShow the evidence behind the suggested order.
03ActRecord a next step within the user's authority.
04TrackMake the response visible without a second report.

The MVP boundary: one case manager's decision workflow, a few meaningful actions and an Operations view based on the same activity. I deliberately left out real integrations, automated legal decisions and the broader case-management platform.

05 · What I Built

The working prototype made priorities, responsibility and follow-through visible.

I used six fictional client cases and seven separate attention needs to demonstrate how the workflow behaves. Alex Rivera is the case manager, Taylor Bennett the attorney and Jamie Torres the paralegal. A fixed demo clock keeps the examples consistent.

One case, two different needs

Avery has a callback commitment requiring attention and a separate document dependency that's appropriately waiting. Neither should overwrite the other.

Correct a fact, not the whole task

Casey's document has arrived, but the receipt checklist hasn't caught up. Confirming receipt clears that discrepancy without completing Jamie's review.

Request isn't acceptance

Devon's attorney review can be requested and tracked, but the request stays pending. The prototype doesn't pretend the attorney has accepted or finished the work.

06 · What Testing Revealed

A simple correction could have hidden a callback that was still owed.

Testing exposed an important flaw in the first interaction model. A general-purpose “correct signal” action could dismiss an outstanding callback commitment, even though the callback had not happened. I changed the UI and underlying logic so recording a future follow-up doesn't make that confirmed commitment disappear from the work needing attention.

Other decisions became more concrete, too. Confirming receipt should not mark a paralegal's review complete. Requesting an attorney's help should not imply acceptance. And Operations should reflect what the case manager recorded, rather than ask the team to update a separate report.

The prototype passed 30 automated functional tests and was checked in a browser. Those tests helped verify the modeled workflows, including the commitment safeguard. They also gave me a more specific set of questions to take into real-user validation.

07 · The Next Investment Decision

I'd validate the workflow with case managers before funding production integrations.

A working demo can test interactions, but the next question is whether the approach improves actual decisions. I'd use a small pilot to examine two outcomes:

Faster, better work decisions

Can case managers identify the right issue, understand it and choose an appropriate response more efficiently than they do today? I'd compare similar scenarios and check decision quality alongside time.

Important work handled on time

Do needs requiring intervention get the appropriate action within an agreed timeframe? I'd audit both surfaced and missed issues, and distinguish a request from accepted or completed work.

I'd also check the reliability of underlying records, permission boundaries, false alerts and performance across a realistic caseload. Those findings would guide the engineering plan for persistence, case-system connections, audit history and follow-up. If the larger problem turned out to be staffing capacity or operating rules, I'd change the investment recommendation rather than build more software.

Closing Perspective

A prototype earns its value by improving the product decision.

I use rapid prototyping to make ideas tangible, expose risky assumptions and decide where engineering effort will make a difference. This example shows how a small working workflow can answer important questions before the team takes on a much larger build.

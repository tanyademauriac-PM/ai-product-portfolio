Work Triage Prototype: Validate Before Engineering Investment

A short path from an ambiguous workflow problem to something people can actually try. I designed a focused work-triage experience, built a functional prototype and used it to make the product decisions—and the unanswered questions—visible before a larger build.

About this case study

This is an independent product-design exercise using a hypothetical legal operations setting. All cases, people, values and activity are fictional. It was built to explore a workflow and test prototype behavior—not deployed for a client or validated with production users. The problem framing and recommended next steps are hypotheses to investigate, not claims about a specific organization.

01 · The problem worth testing

The information exists. Finding the work still takes effort.

Imagine a case manager responsible for roughly 120 active cases. Most are moving along, but the meaningful issues are scattered across notes, tasks, document records and promises made to clients. A case status alone doesn't say which next step deserves attention today.

That creates two related problems: the case manager spends time hunting for work, while an operational leader needs to understand what's happening without asking for separate status updates. My hypothesis was that the gap wasn't simply missing information; it was the step between seeing a case and knowing what to do next.

Who I designed for first

The case manager making daily decisions. Attorneys and paralegals remain the owners of the work they are authorized to perform. Operations gets a read-only view of what has been requested, started, resolved or left waiting.

What still needed validation

Perhaps the real blocker is inconsistent records, unclear operating rules or insufficient capacity. The prototype lets us examine those possibilities before treating another new interface as the answer.

02 · The product choice

Don't stop at a list of exceptions.

I considered a simple list of potentially problematic cases. That would reduce hunting, but it could leave the case manager sorting through yet another queue. I chose a narrower but more complete interaction: show why a specific issue has surfaced, make the available response clear, and record enough follow-through for other roles to see what happened.

01RecognizeSurface an issue, not a vague case status.
02PrioritizeShow evidence and an explainable suggestion.
03ActRecord the next step within the user's authority.
04TrackMake the response visible without a second status report.

My MVP boundary: one case manager's decision journey, a small set of meaningful actions and an Operations view drawing on those same records. No replacement case-management system, automated legal decisions or production integrations.

03 · Try the prototype

Interactive enough to expose the tricky decisions.

The browser demo contains six fictional client cases and seven separately tracked attention needs. The case manager is Alex Rivera, supported by attorney Taylor Bennett and paralegal Jamie Torres. The clock is fixed so the scenarios are predictable.

One case, two different needs

Avery has a callback commitment that needs attention and a separate document dependency that is appropriately waiting. The two must not be treated as one case-level status.

Clear the blockage, not the work

Casey's document has arrived but still appears missing. Confirming receipt fixes that fact without pretending Jamie completed the document review.

Request isn't acceptance

Devon's attorney review can be requested and tracked. The prototype does not claim the attorney accepted or completed it.

Take the three-minute tour

Open the Work view, compare the issues needing attention, confirm Casey's receipt, request Devon's attorney review, then open Operations. Try correcting a signal or recording a future wait. Reset returns the demo to the starting state.

04 · What prototyping gave us

The useful discoveries were in the details.

A clickable workflow forced decisions that a high-level roadmap or mockup could easily leave unresolved. One especially important correction: a generic "incorrect signal" action must not silently dismiss a confirmed outstanding callback, even after the user records a future follow-up. The interaction and underlying logic were adjusted to protect that commitment.

  • Separate attention needs from cases. One waiting dependency doesn't erase another issue that requires action.
  • Keep responsibility honest. Requesting attorney work doesn't mean the attorney has accepted it. The case manager still needs a follow-up.
  • Make the priority understandable. A promise due today can outrank a higher-value case that is appropriately waiting.
  • Do not confuse a correction with completion. Recording an inaccurate signal isn't the same as doing the substantive work.
  • Share one record of the response. The Operations view reflects the same activity rather than relying on a parallel reporting process.

I shaped the problem, scenarios, UX decisions, state boundaries and test expectations, using AI coding assistance to accelerate implementation. The prototype passed 30 automated functional tests and underwent targeted browser checks. Those checks establish that the scripted interactions behaved as intended; they do not establish whether the product improves real-world operations.

05 · What I would validate next

Test the decisions with users before committing to integrations.

Decision speed and quality

Observe case managers reviewing comparable situations today, then measure how efficiently they reach an appropriate next step with the prototype. Check the quality of those decisions, not just the time spent.

Important work handled on time

Measure whether actual intervention needs receive the necessary response within an agreed window. Review unflagged cases for misses, and distinguish a request from acceptance or completed work.

I would also check record reliability, authority rules, false alerts and behavior at a realistic caseload. Only then would I plan durable records, permissions, audit history, case-system updates and automated follow-up with engineering. If the pilot showed that policy or team capacity—not discovery—is the dominant problem, I'd change direction rather than add features.

The point: move quickly, learn what to build, then invest with confidence.

This prototype deliberately does less than a full product. Its value is making the workflow concrete enough to challenge assumptions, identify risky handoffs and decide where production engineering would actually help.
